Every number should be traceable

A digital thread is a connected record. The CAD model, the simulation that used it, the manufacturing notes, where the part is now and what its sensors have felt are all linked together. When someone asks why a call was made, you can follow the trail back. When the field teaches you something, the model gets updated.

We keep the data in open formats so it moves between CAD, CFD, mapping and operations tools without someone retyping it.

Know where the box is and how it has been treated

LuminaBox is a small sensor unit that attaches to a container, a tanker or a vehicle. It records where it is and what it has been through, including shocks, vibration, temperature and humidity, and sends that to a base station. You get a live picture of everything moving around a site.

Put that next to traffic and infrastructure models and you can test ideas before you pour concrete. Would a dedicated truck lane really get fuel to the pads faster? Would more rail?

Fluid simulation that finishes before the decision does

Running CFD on GPUs turns an overnight job into something you can use during planning, or even live. One example is our faster-than-real-time simulation of the air around a vertical Artemis 2 stack, shown here. We use the same approach for ship airwake, exhaust plumes and the turbulent air close to the ground.

One picture of what is in the air

Counter-UAS is mostly a sensor-fusion problem. Radar, cameras and radio sensors each see part of the story, and an operator needs one picture. We combine them, and we add something most systems leave out: how wind, turbulence and nearby buildings change what the sensors can see and when a response can work.

Turning a spaceport into something that adapts

Launches get scrubbed for all kinds of reasons. NASA's rule of thumb is that about one attempt in three doesn't go. LuminaTwin is our answer: a digital twin that combines low-cost hardware with simulation software, so a site can schedule smarter and adjust when something changes. LuminaBox is the hardware that keeps the twin tied to the real site.

Find the traffic jam before you pour the concrete

The planning module looks at how a site really runs today and where things back up. Then it simulates proposed changes, using optimization, discrete-event simulation and AI surrogate models, to find the fixes that buy the most launches for the least money.

Take fuel deliveries at Cape Canaveral. Traffic can hold up the fuel trucks, and it only gets worse as launches become more frequent. With street and rail maps and a traffic simulation we can compare ideas like dedicated truck lanes or more rail and see which ones are worth it.

A forecast for the pad, not for the county

Public weather models work on cells a couple of miles wide. We refine NOAA forecasts down to about a foot, so you know what the wind is doing at a particular crane or building, faster than real time. Managers use that to re-plan the day. The same idea works for a supply-chain hiccup or someone calling in sick.

That is roughly 9,500 times finer in each direction. 1.8 miles is about 9,500 feet.

When the forecast turns, the plan turns with it

LuminaTwin checks every scheduled task against the forecast. If a task would put someone outside in bad weather, it gets swapped for useful indoor work. Nobody is exposed, and nobody stands around waiting.

A small box that tells you how its cargo is doing

LuminaBox clips onto payloads, vehicle sections, containers and tankers, whether they are sitting in storage or on the move. It feels for knocks, vibration, temperature and humidity, tracks its position, and reports to a base station. LuminaTwin uses that feed to see the whole site at once.

Let the trucks tell you which roads are broken

When a fuel tanker with a LuminaBox hits a pothole, the sensor feels the jolt and reports exactly where it happened. Do that across a whole convoy and a repair list writes itself.

A small team that likes hard flow problems

Lumina Flows is a small engineering and technical-services company based in the U.S. Our people have backgrounds in engineering, computing and research software, and we have worked on both government and private-sector projects.

Being small is on purpose. It lets us move quickly and stay flexible, and we would rather build something useful than run a big process. We also care about keeping this kind of technical know-how in the U.S.

Along the way we organize technical conferences and workshops too, because in this field the best ideas usually come from putting the right people in one room.
